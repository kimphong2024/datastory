Teaching a model to hear a poem.

We took an open-source 32-billion-parameter LLM, fed it 259 poems from nine contemporary anthologies, and taught it three jobs: write a poem in a poet's voice, critique a poem on its own terms, and revise a rough draft without smothering it. Two versions, three baselines, one honest reckoning with the ceiling.

01 · The question

Can a small model, shown enough good poetry, write better than a big one?

General-purpose models write competent, generic verse. They've read everything but absorbed nothing in particular. The question was whether a few hundred poems by working contemporary poets — read deeply, with critique attached — could shift a smaller model into a more literary register.

1

The corpus

Nine EPUBs from contemporary poetry collections — Dickinson-heirs, queer anthologies, New Yorker retrospectives, spoken-word chapbooks. Not Gutenberg Victoriana; working voices.

2

The three tasks

Not just "write a poem." Three distinct jobs: write, critique, edit. Each rewards a different skill. A model trained on all three has to hold form, taste, and restraint at once.

3

The teacher

Claude Sonnet 4.6 generated the critiques and synthetic draft corruptions that became the edit-task training data. A good teacher's judgments propagate into the student.

4

The base

Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct. Large enough for nuanced output, small enough to LoRA-tune on a reasonable budget. Together.ai as the hosted training + inference substrate.

5

The honest comparison

A LoRA tune that beats a 7B baseline proves nothing — most of the gap is size. A LoRA tune that beats the same-size base proves the training. And a comparison against Opus 4.7 names the ceiling.

"
The poem on the page is a score. The voice is the music. The fine-tune, then, was an attempt to teach a model the difference between reading notation and hearing what the notation is for.

02 · The pipeline

Seven stages, eight hours, $110 spent.

Start to finish: EPUB parsing to a measurable win-rate against the state of the art, in a single working session. Each stage is independently resumable — distillation in particular is expensive and unreliable if not checkpointed.

Stage 01 · Ingest
EPUBs into raw text, line-breaks preserved

Parse EPUB files with ebooklib. Convert HTML to text while preserving <br> and paragraph boundaries — line-breaks are semantically crucial for poetry. Handle the macOS quirk where .epub files appear as unpacked directories by re-zipping them on the fly.

9 / 9 books ingested · 91,373 lines of raw text
Stage 02 · Clean
Segment books into individual poems

The hard part. Books don't announce where poems start. We use heading heuristics (Roman numerals, all-caps titles), anthology patterns (author-name-then-title blocks), and filters for prose, indices, and front matter. Then we tighten author attribution so "100 Queer Poems" doesn't mark all 91 poems as written by its editor.

259 poems segmented · per-poet attribution correct
Stage 03 · Distill
Teacher generates critique + edit pairs

For each poem, Claude Sonnet 4.6 produces: a theme/form/tone JSON, a 150-250 word critique naming concrete images and prosodic choices, and two plausibly-degraded drafts (one with line-break weakness, one with diction weakness). Plus 30 fresh draft-to-polished pairs on curated themes. All with prompt caching to cut cost.

~820 teacher calls · ~$15 · 2 hours wall time
Stage 04 · Build
Assemble the training JSONL

Each poem yields up to 4 training examples: one WRITE (prompt: style + theme + form, response: the poem), one CRITIQUE (prompt: the poem, response: the critique), two EDIT pairs (prompt: a degraded draft, response: the original + change notes). Fresh-draft pairs add 30 more edits. 95/5 → 10% val split after v2.

918 train · 102 val · balanced across tasks
Stage 05 · Train
LoRA fine-tune on Together.ai

Upload JSONL, launch LoRA training with r=16, alpha=32, 2 epochs, LR 5e-5. 2×H100 SXM provisioned by Together. About 15 minutes of GPU wall time to produce a downloadable adapter and a hosted model endpoint handle.

Job ft-ee3cb453-deb9 · 17 minutes · ~$80
Stage 06 · Eval
Pairwise scoring against three baselines

60 held-out prompts × 3 baselines × 2 generations + 1 judgment = 540 API calls. Baselines: same-size untuned Qwen2.5-32B (temporary dedicated endpoint, auto-torn-down), smaller serverless Qwen2.5-7B, frontier-tier Claude Opus 4.7. Sonnet judges blind on a 5-axis rubric with A/B position-randomised.

180 graded pairs · three reports written
Stage 07 · Serve
Streamlit UI with write / critique / edit tabs

Local web app that talks to the Together.ai inference endpoint. Sidebar toggle for side-by-side comparison against the base model. Reads the latest tuned model from the job metadata and resolves the dedicated endpoint name automatically.

One command: lyrical serve
03 · v1 · what worked and what didn't

A first version that won on paper and told on itself.

The v1 tuned model beat a 7B serverless baseline 78.9% of the time across every axis. Impressive until you read the caveats — and the caveats were doing most of the work.

Five caveats, one uncomfortable truth.

Wins against a 7B model when our tuned model is 32B are mostly about size. Losses clustered in a specific task. Generation failures suggested overfitting. And the judge itself was suspect.

Caveat · 01

The baseline was unfair

Comparing a tuned 32B to a base 7B conflates scale-uplift with training-uplift. We couldn't tell which part of the 78.9% was the LoRA and which was simply four extra billion parameters.

Caveat · 02

Edit was the weakest task

Five of the eight losses were on the EDIT task. The synthetic-degrade method gave thin signal — the model was learning to undo specific corruptions rather than develop revision judgement.

Caveat · 03

Every poem credited one editor

91 poems from "100 Queer Poems" were all labelled as written by its editor, Mary Jean Chan. The model learned to associate one name with 91 different styles — catastrophic overfitting on her prompt pattern.

Caveat · 04

The model looped on itself

At least two generation failures were literal repetition loops — "who is the man, who is the man, who is the man." A known symptom of LoRA overtraining on small corpora at aggressive learning rates.

Caveat · 05

The val set was small

38 held-out examples. Win-rate noise at 95% confidence is roughly ±8 percentage points — so 78.9% could plausibly be anywhere from 70% to 87%. Not precise enough for small axis deltas.

04 · v2 · caveat by caveat

Five fixes. One retraining. All addressable.

Each caveat mapped to a concrete intervention. Some were data fixes (author attribution, dual degradations). Some were training fixes (lower LR, fewer epochs). Some were evaluation fixes (bigger val set, a same-size baseline with auto-provisioned endpoints). The combined v2 pipeline ran in a few hours.

Fix · 01

Auto-provisioned 32B baseline

Eval now spins up a dedicated endpoint for the untuned Qwen2.5-32B, runs comparisons against it, and tears the endpoint down when finished. Plus Opus 4.7 as a frontier ceiling and the 7B for continuity.

3 baselines
Fair comparison, ceiling read
Fix · 02

Dual-degradation + fresh drafts

One teacher call per poem now produces two distinct degraded drafts (line-break corruption + diction weakness). Plus 30 fresh "rough draft → polished" pairs on curated themes, fully synthesized by the teacher.

504 edit examples
2× the v1 edit data
Fix · 03

Per-poet attribution

Tightened anthology-author detection: all-caps, two or more words, no articles at the start. In "100 Queer Poems", each poem now carries its actual author — Colette Bryce, Elizabeth Bishop, Jay Gao — not the anthology editor.

91 names resurfaced
No more style overload
Fix · 04

Gentler training + frequency penalty

Epochs dropped 3 → 2. Learning rate 1e-4 → 5e-5. Inference-side frequency_penalty=0.3 to suppress degenerate repetition at decode time. Smaller update, fewer local minima around the repeat attractor.

0 loops observed
Across 180 v2 generations
Fix · 05

Bigger, balanced val set

Split 95/5 → 90/10. Eval N_HELD_OUT 50 → 60 pairs per baseline. That's 60 × 3 = 180 graded pairs, enough to estimate axis means with usable confidence intervals.

102 val examples
~3× the v1 measurement budget

83% against its same-size sibling.

How to read this

The top row is the only one that matters for proving the tuning worked. The middle row is a sanity check. The bottom row names the ceiling.

Delta values show tuned model mean score minus baseline mean score, on a 1-5 rubric. Positive deltas are uplift for our model; negative are deficit. Each delta represents roughly one axis point of expert literary judgement.

Finding · 01

The tuning is the lift, not the size.

Uplift against the same-family 32B base (+1.10 average across axes) is almost identical to uplift against the 7B (+1.30 average). If size were doing the work, we'd see much bigger gaps against the 7B. We don't. The LoRA training — the teacher's critique patterns, the corpus's voice patterns — is carrying 80%+ of the benefit.

We couldn't have proven this without spinning up a temporary 32B endpoint. The auto-provisioning code paid for itself on the first run.

Finding · 02

Voice is the axis that moved most.

+1.64 on voice vs base-32B — the biggest single delta anywhere in the results. Voice is where absorbed style lives: the particular diction, rhythm, and image-choice of a school of poets. The fine-tune captured the contemporary free-verse register in a way the general-purpose base model simply hadn't encountered at training time.

"Voice" as a rubric axis is the most poetic-judgement-specific signal in the whole eval. That it moved most is a design success.

Finding · 03

Opus 4.7 still wins decisively.

96.7% against us, −2.37 on craft. Opus is 500B-1T parameters of absorbed literary context; our tuned 32B is a specialised middle-tier model. The gap is real and predictable. No LoRA budget can make a 32B dense model write like a frontier-scale model on general poetry prompts. We can close sub-domains — style mimicry of in-corpus poets, critique specificity — but not the whole surface.

Honest framing: LYRICAL is a genuine improvement in its tier. It is not a challenger to the frontier. Those are different products.

Finding · 04

The judge might be biased.

Sonnet 4.6 judging Opus 4.7 vs our model is sibling-judges-sibling. Both share training lineage, stylistic priors, and probably preferences. An unbiased eval would average across judges from different families (GPT-5, Gemini, human raters). We estimate 5-10 percentage points of judge-bias swing — not enough to flip the Opus comparison, but enough to matter for published numbers.

Among the honest caveats to carry forward: our numbers overstate the Opus deficit by some unknown but non-zero amount.

06 · v3 and beyond

How you'd actually try to beat Opus 4.7.

Not hypothetically. With real numbers, real costs, and real returns. Organised into four tiers by cost and effort. The first tier can be tried in an hour; the last tier requires serious investment and still might not get there.

01Tier 1 · inference-side

Best-of-4 with self-critique

Generate 4 at temperature 1.1. Have the model critique each. Pick the best. No retraining.

Cost

~4× inference tokens per prompt. Roughly $0.02 extra per generation.

Expected lift

+0.3-0.5 on craft axis. Pure statistics — mean of best-of-4 is much higher than single-shot mean.

Implementation One afternoonZero retrainingStreamlit-side
02Tier 1 · inference-side

RAG over the corpus

When the prompt names an in-corpus poet, retrieve their actual poems as few-shot context.

Cost

Build cost ~$1 to embed the corpus. Retrieval overhead negligible per query.

Expected lift

+0.6-1.0 on voice for in-corpus authors. Zero lift for unknown authors. Could beat Opus in this narrow domain.

Implementation FAISS indexAuthor-keyedPrompt-template change
03Tier 2 · training-signal

Swap teacher to Opus 4.7

Current teacher is Sonnet 4.6. Upgrade imports Opus-level craft directly into the student.

Cost

Re-distill with Opus. Opus is ~5× Sonnet's price. ~$40-50 for the 259-poem corpus.

Expected lift

+0.3-0.5 on specificity and craft. The teacher's quality ceiling becomes the student's quality ceiling.

Implementation One config lineRe-run distillRe-train v3
04Tier 2 · training-signal

DPO after SFT

Teach the model to discriminate, not just mimic. The single biggest training-side lever.

Cost

Generate 4-8 candidates per prompt, rank with Opus, DPO-train on (winner, loser) pairs. ~$150-250.

Expected lift

+0.5-0.8 across all axes. DPO reliably closes 30-50% of the gap between SFT model and the judge's preferences.

Implementation Candidate genOpus rankingDPO trainer

Corpus 10-20× bigger

259 → 3,000-5,000 curated poems. More styles, more registers, more to absorb.

Cost

~$100-200 for distillation. Curation effort is the actual cost — quality matters more than quantity.

Expected lift

+0.4-0.8 voice and imagery. More poets = more voices to mimic. Non-linear in corpus size.

Achievable sub-wins

Forget "beat Opus overall." These targets are within current reach:

  • → Beat Opus on style imitation of in-corpus poets — with Tier 1 RAG alone.
  • → Beat Opus on critique specificity for formal poetry — with Tier 2 Opus-teacher distillation.
  • → Beat Opus on revision faithful to poet's voice — with Tier 2 DPO.
  • → Beat Opus on any single poem — already achievable 3% of the time.
07 · what this build taught us

Six lessons worth carrying forward.

Beyond the poetry-specific findings — generalisable patterns we'd apply to the next LoRA fine-tuning project.

Lesson · 01

Fair baselines cost money. Pay.

The v1 eval against a 7B baseline was cheap but misleading. Spinning up a same-size 32B endpoint for the v2 eval cost about $3 and completely changed the interpretation. If you can't measure cleanly, you can't improve honestly.

Lesson · 02

The teacher is the ceiling.

A student trained on Sonnet critiques will never write better critiques than Sonnet. Swap in Opus as teacher and the ceiling rises — at 5× the cost. This ratio matters for every distillation project.

Lesson · 03

Metadata errors compound.

Tagging 91 poems as written by one editor didn't just fail one prompt — it degraded every "write in the style of X" example for that editor's name. In fine-tuning, small data-quality issues are not small.

Lesson · 04

Silent failures are the dangerous ones.

Our v1 distillation silently dropped ~65% of attempted poems because a JSON field came back as a list instead of a string. Zero error messages, zero alerts — just missing files. Default stdout buffering hid the problem until we forced a flush.

If your loop has a try/except, make the except verbose and the prints flushed. Always.

Lesson · 05

Overfitting shows up as repetition.

Two v1 write outputs were literal infinite loops. Classic LoRA overfitting signature. The combination of 3 epochs at LR 1e-4 on a 727-example corpus was too aggressive. v2 at 2 epochs + 5e-5 eliminated it — plus inference-side frequency_penalty as belt-and-braces.

Lesson · 06

Infrastructure state is part of the problem.

Together.ai's "Unable to access non-serverless model" errors, the mismatch between a fine-tune's model field and the callable endpoint name, the leaked endpoint after an unexpected failure — none of these were in the model-quality category. They were infrastructure quirks that cost real time to diagnose.

Half of a production ML pipeline is understanding the platform. Budget for it.

The bottom line

A specialised 32B model can write more like a poet than a generic 32B model. It cannot yet write as well as Opus 4.7.

LYRICAL is now running, reachable via a Streamlit app and a Together.ai endpoint. Every caveat from v1 has been addressed in v2, with the caveats named, the fixes labelled, and the measurement bar raised. The ceiling against a frontier-scale model is honest, diagnosable, and has a ranked roadmap to close.

The project lives at github.com/kimphong2024/lyrical. The pipeline is documented, the training data is reproducible, and the adapter is a single downloadable artefact. Next session, if we want to close the Opus gap by another 30-40%, we know exactly where to start.
